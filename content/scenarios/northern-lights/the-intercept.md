# The Intercept, in Numbers

**GM reference — the burn math behind Scene 1.** The scene plays without any of this; reach for it only when a player wants the numbers, or use the boarding threat clock below to pace the CSA approach.

## The burn

The crew's vessel crosses the intercept diagonal on a constant-thrust brachistochrone — burn to the midpoint, flip, brake to a velocity match.

```
INTERCEPT SOLUTION — NJORD'S WHISPER
Range at commit:        1.2 AU
Profile:                Full burn / flip / full brake
Acceleration:           3.0 g sustained, nano-med protocol
Time of flight:         43 h 30 m
Flip:                   H+21:45 after launch
Peak velocity:          2,300 km/s
Tracking solution:      DEGRADES H+20:00
Updates after H+20:     NONE. Dead reckoning only.
```

The physics, for the table's engineers: *t = 2·√(d/a)*. At 3 g over 1.2 AU that is a shade under 44 hours, half of it decelerating. The total delta-v is obscene — this is what the fuel tanks are *for* — and the crew arrives near-dry: no margin for a second hard approach, just enough left for a slow, low-thrust crawl home. **One pass. That's the job.**

## Two facts the burn creates

1. **They are blind.** Tracking dies at H+20, and the flip is not until H+21:45. The crew goes dark on new data *while still accelerating* — the flip itself, the most important maneuver of the trip, runs on dead reckoning. If the Whisper had changed course, they would find out by arriving at empty space with dry tanks. (She hasn't. They can't know that.)
2. **The fast profile is loud — but loud is a choice, not a law.** A full brachistochrone brake points the fusion flare at the destination for nearly twenty-two hours; anything aboard the Whisper that can see knows *something* decelerated toward her, roughly where and roughly when. That is the price of arriving fast. A crew willing to spend time can fly it differently: brake early and off-axis while still far out, cut the torch, and coast the final stretch cold and dark on a matched vector, radiators turned away. For the final approach, the pilot must establish a hidden vector behind the main engine, where its structure masks the point-defense arcs. Low emissions help; they do not make the vessel invisible. **Ghost trades time and fuel margin for a chance at that concealed coast.** Detection turns the exhaust corridor into a threat and forces a new crossing plan. What no one can buy back is the coarse fact that *something* came this way; what the crew controls is whether the Whisper ever gets a clean, targetable picture of them.

## The deadline is made of rocks

The Whisper keeps burning her freight profile the whole time the crew is in transit. Contact happens at ~2.05 AU — the doorstep of the Belt. Two days later she is inside it: a dark ship under quiet thrust behind a million radar echoes, and no solution ever finds her again. This intercept threads the last clean stretch of empty space she will ever cross.

## The second solution

The crew's burn is not the only solution threaded on this clock. It is just the loud one.

The CSA never needed six days of quiet cross-referencing to act, because the CSA never cared what she was carrying. The undeclared rendezvous itself — **Day 12**, the same watch-list ping everyone got — is the whole legal case: a transport that takes on unknown cargo in deep space forfeits her protections, and seizing her stops being piracy and becomes *extraction*. While the lesser submind was still connecting dots, an NHA flight was already loading. No job posting, no escrow, no crew to assemble: the marines were the standby roster, and the interceptor was already fueled on a picket.

```
INTERCEPT SOLUTION — CSA NHA FLIGHT
Flag received:       Day 12 — the rendezvous itself. No analysis required.
                     (THESEUS needed six more days to learn WHAT she carried.
                     The CSA only needed to know that she'd forfeited.)
Asset:               Military interceptor, Mars-trailing picket volume,
                     0.86 AU from the projected merge point. Marines aboard.
Launch:              Day 13. One day of spin-up, most of it legal review.
Profile:             Burn / coast / merge. No brake leg. None.
Burn:                4 g for 76 minutes — all of it 0.86 AU out,
                     flare pointed away from her.
Coast:               180 km/s. 8¼ days. Torch cold, radiators trailing.
Merge:               Around Day 21 — terminal approach paced by the story clock.
Relative velocity:   ~0. By design, not by burning.
Terminal trim:       < 2 km/s. Cold gas and one ninety-second squirt.
Tanks at merge:      Nearly full.
```

The physics, for the table's engineers — and this one is *pretty*: the Whisper never stops her ten-milligee freight burn, so her velocity climbs a steady ~8.5 km/s per day. If you know early enough, you do not brake at all. Pick a coasting velocity *V*, and the merge point solves itself: *t = V/a* — the moment her own curve climbs up to your speed. Arrive there ballistic, and **the target executes your velocity match for you**. Zero-relative-velocity rendezvous, and the only torch ever lit was a 76-minute burn nine days earlier, most of an AU away, pointed somewhere else.

### Why the crew could never fly this

Three stacked reasons, and none of them is skill:

1. **Information.** The merge needs a launch by ~Day 14 to catch her curve at 180 km/s before the rocks. The crew's employer didn't finish *understanding the problem* until Day 19. The CSA moved on the flag; THESEUS moved on the answer.
2. **Basing.** 0.86 AU from a standing picket versus 1.2 AU from wherever the gig economy parked them — with a tracking solution that died 20 hours after it was born. Distance plus a dead clock forces speed.
3. **The dial.** Fact #2 above — invisibility and speed are one dial turned opposite ways. The CSA had enough *time* to buy invisibility. The crew spent everything they had buying speed, and the price tag was a 21-hour brake flare pointed straight at her.

And the uncomfortable footnote: the CSA *could* have arrived first. A 4-g brachistochrone from the picket is 1.3 days — they could have been aboard by Day 14, a week before anyone. Doctrine said no. A military torch screaming onto a flagged transport in the monitored inner volume is piracy on camera; NHA work is *deniable*, and deniable means grabbing her at the Belt doorstep, dark, at the last clean moment the rocks allow. They picked the latest, quietest merge on the board. Both crews converge on the same final stretch of empty space. These approximate transit figures explain how they got here; the boarding threat clock governs the playable approach.

### What everyone sees

| Observer | Of the crew | Of the CSA |
|---|---|---|
| **The Whisper** | A fusion flare decelerating toward her for 21¾ hours. Coarse, no track after a successful Ghost approach — but *unmissable*. | A 76-minute burn nine days ago, 0.86 AU away, pointed elsewhere. Filed under traffic by everything that saw it. Functionally: nothing. |
| **The crew** | — | A low-priority contact, if anything at all — and here is the cruel part: the merge profile is *designed* to read as traffic. No closing burn, no intercept geometry, velocity matched to the lane — every threat filter ever written files that as a transport sharing the corridor. The assistant is not failing; it is being correctly, professionally wrong. If the crew chose the Active sweep in Scene 1 and keeps the radar hot through the boarding, the assistant holds a track the whole time — filed under *transit traffic, no intercept geometry* — and a maintained watch can expose the committed approach at 9:00 instead of waiting for the close-contact report at 10:00. That warning buys an opportunity to act; it does not dictate the outcome. |
| **The CSA** | The whole show. They spent their coast watching a fusion flare decelerate onto *their* salvage claim, and they merged already briefed: the claim is occupied, professionals aboard, already working the target. The marines do not knock. | — |

One more asymmetry, and it matters in act two: **delta-v**. The crew spent ~4,600 km/s getting here and arrives near-dry — a slow crawl home is the entire remaining budget. The CSA spent 182. Their tanks are effectively full. If it comes to running, nobody outruns them; if it comes to chasing, they have the fuel to be patient about it.

## Boarding threat clock

**GM pacing tool. Start at 3:00 when the first contractor gets inside the Whisper.** A scout on the outer hull has not started it. The labels track how far the CSA assault has developed, not hours since contact. The brig launched days ago; nothing the contractors open or transmit summons it.

| Stage | What the CSA does | What can become visible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **3:00 — Closing** | The brig coasts toward the occupied target. | A functioning sensor watch may hold an unremarkable traffic contact. No attack has been identified. |
| **6:00 — Ready** | The boarding team secures its equipment; the brig refines the terminal solution. | A maintained track shows the contact staying with the lane. The preparations aboard it remain unseen. |
| **9:00 — Committed** | The brig commits to the assault approach. | Maintained sensor watch can distinguish the closing geometry from routine traffic and warn the crew. |
| **10:00 — Close aboard** | The assault brig closes into the final approach. | With working sensors, the assistant reclassifies the contact. Use the report below and the assessment in The Second Boarding. |
| **11:00 — Final alignment** | The brig lines up on its chosen impact area. | The ram becomes clear to anyone with the necessary view or report. Resolve immediate movement, cover and last interventions. |
| **12:00 — Impact** | The brig reaches the target. | Resolve the ram and boarding, or the changed outcome the crew's intervention earned. |

### Advance at a junction

At a decision point, establish what the crew can see and what remains unresolved. Give them room to act. If they circle the same argument, wait without committing to a course, or abandon a problem while expecting everything else to hold still, advance **one stage** and put its development into the fiction. Use junctions such as choosing a route after entry, deciding what to do with surrendered spacers, or deciding whether to open the cargo assembly. Do not predetermine their answer.

A first argument is not automatically a stall. Questions needed to understand the situation, reading a handout, resolving an action, or taking a real-world pause do not advance this clock. There is no tick per roll, failed check, player, or page. Do not charge the same unresolved pause repeatedly before presenting the changed situation and giving the crew another opportunity to act.

A tick changes the CSA's situation even when nobody can observe it. Report only evidence available through actual sightlines, sensors, and communication. A broken sensor array does not manufacture the assistant's warning; a disconnected operator does not hear it until someone gets a message through. Track who knows what. If an action directly establishes a later stage, record that stage rather than replaying earlier ones.

### At the lab breach

When the first contractor enters Origin-17's connected living/lab module assembly, retire the boarding-stall mechanism. That includes a workable improvised entry. **Bring final approach to at least 10:00.** If the clock was at 3:00, 6:00 or 9:00, it is now 10:00; issue the close-contact report if the crew has the means to receive it. If it was already at 10:00 or 11:00, retain that stage and every warning or action already resolved. If impact has happened, it stays happened.

The lab and final approach now run alongside one another. The crew may face the intrusion, resist it, block the transmission, accept or refuse the orders, or move to defend the ship. None of those decisions is a required tick. Give the crew a meaningful response to the warning, then develop the brig's final alignment at 11:00 and resolve its closing run at 12:00 using [The Second Boarding](/scenarios/northern-lights/the-second-boarding/). Do not wait for a successful override, and do not advance once per hack while resolving the lab.

### When the crew changes the threat

Advance can reach 12:00 before the lab. Run the warning and assault where the crew actually is; nobody is moved into the modules to keep the scene order tidy. A fast crew reaching the lab below 10:00 gets the handoff, not a retroactive expenditure of fictional hours.

The clock describes an assault that can be changed. If the crew disables the brig, forces another course, or otherwise makes a stage impossible, resolve that result and revise or retire the remaining stages. Lab entry does not resurrect a defeated or diverted threat. Preserve warnings, preparations, damage and positions already earned. The unfinished boarding fight still needs its own encounter material; the clock does not supply missing combat statistics.

### The contact report

The reveal is the assistant's, and it comes in as a correction, not an alarm — because for eight days there was nothing to be alarmed about. Something like:

> **"Contact reclassified. Close aboard. Assessed type: stealth assault brig. Probable CSA. Identification unconfirmed."**

At 10:00, run the assessment and preparation window in [The Second Boarding](/scenarios/northern-lights/the-second-boarding/). The first report identifies a suspected vessel type, not its boarding plan. The brig is committing to a ram; recognizing that early is the crew’s opportunity.

That is the whole horror of the merge in one line: the assistant did its job perfectly. A contact sharing the lane, matched vector, no intercept burn — *transit traffic*, filed and forgotten eight days ago. Maintained watch can catch the approach at 9:00; otherwise the close range at 10:00 forces the reclassification, provided the sensors still work. It did not need an obvious closing burn. Its terminal trim is enough to turn a quiet contact into a boarding threat.

**How the ping lands depends entirely on how they came in — and that is the Scene 1 choice cashing out:**

- **They completed Ghost and preserved concealment.** Then they are *also* the needle in the haystack — dark, cold, matched to the Whisper. The CSA merged briefed on a fusion flare, not on a boarding party they can resolve; the crew's own stealth is now the thing buying them the only edge they'll get. The ping is a gift of minutes: kill the lights, hold still, and decide whether to fight from concealment, hide aboard, or be somewhere the marines aren't. Ghost bought quiet on the way in and it buys quiet now — symmetry the players earned.
- **They came in loud (Ram, or a failed Ghost approach followed by a fight through the defenses).** Then the CSA merged onto a hull that is lit, warm, and unmistakably occupied. The boarding has left evidence. The marines can see the breach and exposed activity, but bulkheads still conceal individual positions. The crew can use that geometry, their preparations, and whatever they have learned about what is aboard.

The clock brings the threat forward. The crew’s approach determines what cover, concealment, working equipment and warning they have when it arrives.

If you want the reveal to sting twice, let the assistant re-run its own archive after the fact: *"Seventy-six minutes of four-gravity burn. Nine days ago. Mars-trailing volume. I classified it as traffic."* Beat. *"It was not traffic."*

A detected active sweep alone does not establish a damaged hull or an exposed boarding party. Carry forward what the crew actually did after the challenge, and what the CSA can observe.
